THE ORDINANCE OF THE RECKONING

On that day the night decreases and amounts to nine parts, and the day to nine parts, and the night is equal to the day and the year is exactly as to its days three hundred and sixty-four.
— ḤANOK 72:32, the last day of the twelfth month
Command the children of Yasharal that they observe the years according to this counting, three hundred and sixty-four days, and these will constitute a complete year, and they will not disturb its time from its days and from its feasts; they will not leave out any day nor disturb any feasts.
— YOḆELIM 6:32
How this calendar is reckoned. The sun's sign is watched at Yarushalayim: the day when the night is made equal to the day (about the sixteenth or seventeenth of March by the reckoning of the nations). That equal day is the 364th day, the last of the year (ḤANOK 72:32). The new year then begins on the fourth day of the week that follows it — the day the lights were set in the expanse — and because the year is two and fifty whole weeks, every appointed time keeps its own weekday forever. Within every year all 364 days are counted; none is left out and none is added (YOḆELIM 6:30–32), and the week of seven days is never broken. In most years the 364th day lands beside the next equal day and the years join seamlessly. When the count would outrun the sun, the last week is followed by days of watching — a whole week outside the year, in which the turning of the sun is awaited — so that no Shabbath is ever moved and no day of the year is ever misnamed. The books themselves command the count and the sign but are silent on how the two are to be joined across many years; the days of watching are this calendar's answer, and they are shown plainly, never hidden.
And all the days of the mitsvah will be two and fifty weeks of days, and these will make the entire year complete. Thus it is engraved and ordained on the heavenly tablets.
